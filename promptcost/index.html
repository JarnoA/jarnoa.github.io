1 · When a young person asks

"How much water does AI use?"

The honest answer: nobody knows exactly. And that is a good place to start.

💧 Where the water goes

AI runs in data centres. Many of them cool their servers with water, and part of that water evaporates.

🏢 Why the numbers differ

It depends on the model, the data centre, the climate and the time of year. And companies decide what they publish.

🔁 "But isn't it a closed loop?"

Some data centres cool with a closed loop that reuses the same water. They use much less water on site — but they need more electricity for cooling, and making electricity often uses water too, at power plants somewhere else. Many large data centres still cool by evaporation: that water goes up as steam and comes back as rain, but not to the same town's taps. So the honest answer is: it depends on how it is built. That is why it is worth asking.

Why not just give the number?

A number alone makes a young person feel guilty about one prompt, while the big decisions are made somewhere else. In the 2000s, the oil company BP made the term "carbon footprint" popular to move attention from companies to individuals. Let's not do their work for them.

Say instead: "Nobody knows exactly. Let's find out together."
What young people in Europe say

40,000 young people answered

UNICEF asked young people in 21 countries in Europe and Central Asia (UNICEF & ETF, From Awareness to Action, 2024).

47.7 %

say there are not enough opportunities to express their views on environmental policies and climate action. Another 19.3 % don't know what opportunities exist.

→ You can show them.
61.3 %

have little or no confidence that their government will keep its climate promises. 20.3 % don't know what was promised.

→ Finding out is a good first task.
68.5 %

get climate information mainly from social media; 51.1 % from schools.

→ Check sources together.
14.6 %

name business and industry as responsible for tackling climate change.

→ Zoom out: who builds, who pays, who decides?

Will technological fixes alone solve the climate crisis? Young people are split.

30 % yes30 % not sure40 % no
→ AI is not only a problem or only a solution. Ask what it is used for.
2 · The three bags

Not "use less AI"

That tells her what to stop and gives her nothing to carry. Here are three bags to carry with her. Each bag is one session you can run.

Your carbon handprint

A footprint is the harm you cause. A handprint is the good you help others do. Youth work has a big handprint: when you help a group of young people ask, zoom out and find another way, the change does not stay with you — it spreads.

Bag 1

Ask why

Don't answer with a number. Ask back:

"Why do you ask?"

Her question is often not about water. It is about caring. Researcher Maria Ojala: worry is not the problem — worry is caring. Hope that grows from action helps young people; hope that says "everything will be fine" does not.

Session plan · 45 min
Ages 12+Any group size
  1. 10 minEveryone writes one question they have about AI and nature. No wrong questions.
  2. 10 minIn pairs: Why did you ask that? What do you care about behind the question?
  3. 15 minAsk an AI the same question. Then check the answer together: Where does this come from? Who could know better? What does the AI not say?
  4. 10 minCircle: one thing I care about, one thing I want to find out.

You need: paper, one device with an AI chat, the question cards.

Why do you ask?
What worries you?
What would you like to be different?
Who could know the answer?
How could we check it?
Bag 2

Zoom out

One question is small. The building behind it is not. Four questions for any data centre:

What is it for? Who pays? Who decides? Whose river?

"But the water comes back." It does — as rain, somewhere else. Not to the same town's taps, because much of it evaporates. Even when some water is returned, large withdrawals can strain rivers and groundwater (UNU-INWEH 2026).

Session plan · 45 min
Ages 13+Groups of 3–4
  1. 10 minFind the nearest data centre on a map. Search "data centre" + your region, or use a public data-centre map.
    Example from Finland: Yle article about data-centre locations (in Finnish).
  2. 20 minIn groups, find answers to the four questions. Use news, the company's page, the town's page. Write down what you could not find. That is also a result.
  3. 15 minShare: What surprised you? Which question was hardest to answer, and why?

You need: devices with internet, a big paper with the four questions.

Warm-up idea: make memes about AI's environmental cost — see Meme-tivism under Stories.

Bag 3

Ask for another way

Remember the ozone hole? We didn't stop cooling. We changed the gas. The fridges kept working.

Data centres don't have to cool themselves with drinking water. There are other ways. None of them is free. But it's a choice. Help young people ask the people who make the choice:

"Could you do it another way?"

They don't always listen. That's why we ask together, and in public.

Session plan · 45–90 min
Ages 12+Small groups
  1. 15 minTake photos of one problem in your own street or area. (Young people in Colombia did this: trash, standing water.)
  2. 15 minAsk an AI for three strengths, three weaknesses and five questions about your idea for fixing it.
  3. 15 minFind out what your town or government has promised on climate. Then write one question to someone who decides — with the question builder.
  4. optionalSend it. Agree who follows up, and when.

You need: phones for photos, one device with an AI chat.

    4 · Stories
    
What people did

    
What they did, what changed — and what you can borrow.

    


      

        Montevideo, Uruguay · 2023
        
The salty taps

        
The city almost ran out of fresh water; tap water turned salty. At the same time, Google planned a data centre that would cool itself with 7.6 million litres of drinking water a day — the daily household water of 55,000 people. People took to the streets:

        
"No es sequía, es saqueo."
It's not drought, it's plunder.

        
What changedGoogle redesigned the data centre so it would not cool itself with drinking water.

        
BorrowThe slogan. Name the problem in five words.

        DW · Mongabay
      


      

        Muhos & Kajaani, Finland · 2026
        
Forest first, questions later

        
Google cut down over 500 hectares of forest — about 700 football fields — at its planned data-centre sites before the environmental assessment was finished. The authority asked Google to stop the work.

        
What changedThe work has to wait for the assessment, and Google has to answer questions. Will they continue? Probably. That is what asking does: it makes them answer in public.

        
BorrowFollow your local news and ask: was the assessment done first?

        Yle, 8.10.2026
      


      

        Germany · 2021
        
Young people went to court

        
Young people took the climate law to court — and won. The Constitutional Court said pushing emission cuts past 2030 puts the burden on the young, and ordered the law to be fixed.

        
What changedThe law had to be fixed.

        
BorrowYoung people have rights here. The UN says states must listen to children in climate and environment decisions (General Comment No. 26, 2023).

        Neubauer et al. v Germany, Federal Constitutional Court
      


      

        Cartagena, Colombia · 2026
        
Cameras, AI and the people who decide

        
Twelve teenage citizen scientists in El Pozón photographed environmental health problems in their own streets: trash, standing water. They used AI to test their ideas — three strengths, three weaknesses, five questions — and took them to the people who decide.

        
Outcome38 decision-makers came. They committed to bins inside the school, lids for the open holes the students had drawn with AI, and dengue workshops. Follow-up was short and informal, so lasting change isn't proven. AI worked best as a thinking aid, not as the decider.

        
BorrowThe whole method — it's Bag 3.

        De la Vega-Taboada et al., JMIR AI 2026
      


      

        Quilicura, Chile · 2026
        
A town became a "human AI"

        
Quilicura, near Santiago, is a data-centre hub in a long drought. For one Saturday, residents ran Quili.ai: a chatbot answered by people, not machines. The project says it answered over 25,000 prompts from 68 countries.

        
OutcomeNo measured change. It made the hidden water cost of AI visible around the world.

        
BorrowPlayful, public, together. quili.ai

        AP, 31.1.2026 · Corporación NGEN
      


      

        London & Berlin · 2024–2026
        
Meme-tivism

        
Workshops where people make memes about the environmental cost of AI, with prompt cards and meme templates. Humour makes a heavy topic easier to talk about — and powerlessness easier to name.

        
BorrowA meme session as a warm-up for Bag 2.

        Sinem Görücü & Georgia Panagiotidou · Climate Action Tech
